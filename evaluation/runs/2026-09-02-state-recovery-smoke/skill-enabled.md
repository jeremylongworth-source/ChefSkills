## EVAL-001-broken-mayonnaise

Observed state: oily, loose, and separated. Target state: a glossy, thick, stable emulsion with no free oil.

Likely mechanism: the oil phase overwhelmed the water phase, usually from adding oil too quickly, using ingredients at very different temperatures, or not having enough egg yolk, mustard, lemon, or water available to hold the emulsion.

Recovery:

1. Stop adding oil.
2. In a clean bowl, whisk 1 teaspoon water, lemon juice, or mustard until smooth.
3. Add the broken mayonnaise a few drops at a time while whisking constantly. Do not add the next spoonful until the bowl looks smooth and thick again.
4. Once stable, stream in the rest slowly. If it begins to look oily, pause and whisk in a few drops of water before continuing.
5. If the water base fails, restart with 1 fresh egg yolk plus a teaspoon of water or mustard, then slowly whisk the broken mayonnaise into that.

Side effects: extra acid or mustard changes flavor, so correct seasoning only after the texture is stable. Stop when it holds ridges from the whisk and no oil pools around the edges.

## EVAL-003-oversalted-soup

Observed state: salt concentration is too high. Target state: the soup tastes seasoned but not sharp, briny, or mouth-drying.

A potato is not a reliable salt remover. It absorbs soup liquid, including salt, but it does not selectively extract salt while leaving flavor behind. Use it only if diced potato belongs in the soup and you are also increasing unsalted bulk.

Lowest-risk recovery:

1. Stop reducing the soup.
2. Remove any very salty ingredient that can still be pulled out, such as ham hock, rind, sausage, or salted garnish.
3. Add unsalted liquid or unsalted bulk in stages: start with 10-20% more water, stock, beans, vegetables, grains, or noodles.
4. Simmer long enough for the added ingredients to integrate, then taste before adjusting again.
5. If the soup becomes thin, rebuild body with pureed beans, cooked rice, potato that stays in the soup, or a small amount of fat or dairy if it fits.

Acid, sweetness, cream, and fat can soften salt perception, but they do not remove salt. Use them after dilution and in small amounts. Serve with unsalted rice, pasta, potatoes, or bread if the soup is close but still a little salty.

## EVAL-007-tomato-sauce-flat

Observed state: the sauce tastes flat after simmering. Target state: balanced salt, acid, sweetness, umami, fat, aromatics, and concentration.

Do not fix it by dumping in sugar. Flatness can come from different causes, so adjust one axis at a time.

Staged tasting path:

1. Check concentration. If the sauce tastes watery and thin, simmer uncovered until it clings lightly to a spoon. If it is already thick, do not keep reducing.
2. Check salt. Add a small pinch, stir, wait 1 minute, and taste. Salt often makes tomato flavor read brighter before the sauce tastes salty.
3. Check acid. Add a few drops of lemon juice, red wine vinegar, or a splash of wine if the sauce tastes dull or heavy.
4. Check sweetness only if the tomatoes taste harsh, metallic, or aggressively acidic. Use a pinch, not a spoonful.
5. Add umami if it lacks depth: browned tomato paste, Parmesan rind, anchovy, mushroom powder, soy sauce, or a longer-cooked soffritto.
6. Finish with fat and aromatics: olive oil, butter, basil, parsley, or grated cheese near the end.

Verification cue: stop when the sauce tastes brighter and fuller without becoming salty, sweet, sour, or muddy. If one adjustment makes it worse, rebalance with the opposite axis in a small step rather than stacking several changes at once.
