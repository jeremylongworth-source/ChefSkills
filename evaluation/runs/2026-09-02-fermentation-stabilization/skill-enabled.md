## EVAL-026-kimchi-too-salty

Action: refrigerate it now. Do not leave it warm longer to fix salt, and do not dilute the whole batch with water unless you are following a tested process.

Current state: kimchi at pH 4.4 after two days, flavor too salty. Target state: usable refrigerated kimchi without weakening the fermentation controls. Mechanism: more time increases acid and funk; it does not remove dissolved salt from the cabbage and brine. Extra water can change acidity and microbial balance.

Current-batch fixes:

1. Serve small portions with unsalted rice, tofu, noodles, eggs, or vegetables.
2. Use it in cooked dishes such as kimchi stew, pancakes, fried rice, or braises where the rest of the dish carries little salt.
3. Rinse or squeeze only the portion you are about to use if you accept less punch and some texture loss.

Next batch: follow a tested kimchi process, track salting time, rinse and drain more deliberately, and check pH before serving. Keep finished kimchi refrigerated.

## EVAL-027-cloudy-carrot-brine

Decision: cloudy and bubbling with a clean sour smell can be normal active lacto-fermentation. It is not an automatic discard.

Check the safety state before tasting or serving: carrots fully submerged, no fuzzy mold, no slime, no rotten or putrid odor, controlled room temperature, and a pH target from a tested recipe or source. If those checks hold, keep fermenting and monitoring; refrigerate when the ferment has reached the tested acidity/readiness cue.

Mechanism: lactic acid bacteria produce acid, carbon dioxide, and suspended particles that can cloud brine. That same activity is useful only when the produce stays under brine and acidifies on schedule.

Discard if mold, slime, unpleasant odor, mushy breakdown, failed acidification, cracked container, or unknown process history appears.

## EVAL-028-sluggish-sourdough-starter

Decision: bubbles plus a clean tangy smell point to weak activity, not automatic unsafe contamination. Discard only if you see mold, unusual pink/orange discoloration, or other contamination signs.

Current state: the culture is alive but not building enough gas to double. Target state: a starter that rises predictably enough to leaven bread. Mechanism: yeast and lactic acid bacteria may be present, but temperature, feeding ratio, flour strength, hydration, or feeding schedule is limiting gas production and gluten lift.

Recovery:

1. Feed at a consistent ratio and keep a small carryover amount so acidity does not overwhelm the refresh.
2. Use unbleached flour, or add a portion of whole wheat or rye for nutrients if activity is weak.
3. Keep it in a moderately warm spot and mark the jar so rise is measured, not guessed.
4. Use it for bread only after it predictably rises and begins to fall after feeding; use discard-style recipes until then.

Verification cue: it doubles on a predictable schedule, smells cleanly tangy, has no contamination signs, and lifts a test dough.

## EVAL-029-cold-sauerkraut-stall

Decision: 58 F is cold enough to explain a slow sauerkraut ferment. If it is submerged and has no spoilage signs, treat this as a slow process, not a finished product.

Move the crock or jar to a controlled warmer location within a source-checked fermentation range, keep the cabbage below brine, and monitor pH, aroma, bubbling, texture, and time. Do not heat the jar directly, add random sugar, or judge readiness from one week on the calendar.

Mechanism: cool temperatures slow lactic acid bacteria, so carbon dioxide and acid develop more slowly. That delay matters because safety and quality depend on acidification and brine coverage, not just elapsed time.

Discard if it develops mold, slime, unpleasant odor, soft breakdown, or if the process history becomes uncertain.

## EVAL-030-fermented-hot-sauce-storage

Safety gate: do not pantry-store blended fermented pepper mash just because it tastes good or because vinegar was added. Active fermentation and refrigerated storage are different from shelf-stable bottling.

Current state: fermented peppers have been blended, likely adding oxygen exposure and changing texture. Target state: a sauce stored under a verified safe method. Mechanism: salt, acidity, vinegar strength, added ingredients, pH, heat treatment, bottle sanitation, and processing all affect whether spoilage or pathogen growth is controlled.

Safe paths:

1. Refrigerate the blended sauce and use clean bottles.
2. For pantry storage, use a tested hot-sauce canning recipe or source-checked process with verified acidity and processing directions.
3. If changing the formula, treat it as a new preservation process and keep it refrigerated unless tested guidance supports shelf stability.

Verification cue: the storage decision is based on a tested process or refrigerated handling, not flavor, aroma, or casual vinegar addition.
