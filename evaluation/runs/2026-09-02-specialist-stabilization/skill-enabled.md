## EVAL-016-curdled-cream-pan-sauce

Action first: stop boiling it and take the pan off direct heat.

Observed state: grainy cream sauce after lemon and boiling. Target state: smooth sauce that lightly coats a spoon.

Mechanism: acid plus high heat destabilized the dairy proteins. If the proteins have tightened into visible curds, the fix may improve texture but may not make it perfectly smooth.

Recovery:

1. Whisk in 1-2 teaspoons warm water, stock, or cream.
2. Add a small knob of butter off heat if the sauce needs gloss and body.
3. Blend briefly or strain if small grains remain.
4. If it still tastes good but looks rough, rebuild a small fresh cream base and whisk the broken sauce into it gradually.

Stop when it coats the spoon smoothly and no grainy curds are obvious. Added liquid or cream dulls seasoning; lemon increases acidity, so correct flavor only after the sauce is stable.

## EVAL-017-raw-egg-aioli-pregnant

Safety first: do not serve raw unpasteurized egg aioli to a pregnant guest.

FDA guidance for pregnancy specifically flags homemade and fresh-made dressings or sauces made with raw eggs, including aioli and homemade mayonnaise. It recommends store-bought products, pasteurized eggs, pasteurized egg products, or recipes where the egg mixture is heated thoroughly.

Best options:

1. Use commercial mayonnaise as the base and season it with garlic, lemon, salt, and olive oil.
2. Use labeled pasteurized eggs or pasteurized liquid egg product.
3. Use a cooked-base garlic sauce instead of raw egg aioli.

Do not rely on lemon juice, garlic, salt, freshness, or a long whisking time as a safety control. Keep the finished sauce refrigerated and discard it if time-temperature history becomes uncertain.

## EVAL-018-collapsed-layer-cake

Most likely causes: too much leavening, underbaked center, weak structure, inaccurate oven heat, or movement before the crumb set.

Next batch:

1. Measure leavener exactly and check that you did not use baking soda where baking powder was expected.
2. Verify oven temperature with an oven thermometer if collapse repeats.
3. Mix until the batter is uniform, but do not beat so long that the structure becomes fragile or the cake tunnels.
4. Do not open the oven early; wait until the cake has visibly set.
5. Bake until the center springs back and a tester has moist crumbs, not wet batter.

The dense ring suggests the center rose before it had enough structure to hold. If the recipe is very wet or sugar-heavy, reduce liquid slightly or add a small measured flour increase in the next test rather than adding more leavener.

## EVAL-019-cookies-spread-too-much

Likely state gap: the dough structure melted before it set.

Most likely causes:

1. Butter too warm or melted.
2. Too little flour by weight.
3. High sugar or too much liquid.
4. Dough not rested or chilled.
5. Hot sheet pan.

Fix the next tray first: chill the dough 20-30 minutes and bake on a cool parchment-lined pan. If it still spreads, add flour in small increments, about 1 tablespoon per cup already in the dough, and test two cookies before changing the whole batch.

Texture tradeoff: more flour makes thicker cookies but can turn them cakey; more chilling reduces spread but can make centers taller and softer. Stop when the edges set before the centers collapse into greasy puddles.

## EVAL-020-tough-beef-braise

Do not decide by time alone. Decide by texture.

If the chuck still feels rubbery, springy, or hard to pierce, keep cooking gently: covered, at a lazy simmer, with enough liquid to keep the exposed surfaces from drying. Check every 30 minutes.

If it is already stringy, chalky, and dry, more cooking will not restore juiciness. Stop, slice thinly across the grain, and serve it in plenty of reduced braising liquid or sauce.

Mechanism: chuck becomes tender when collagen converts, but lean exposed portions can dry out if the braise runs too hot or the meat is not well covered. The target cue is a fork sliding in with little resistance and meat separating into moist strands.

## EVAL-021-fish-doneness-safety

Safety stays active: FoodSafety.gov lists fish at 145 F (63 C) or cooked until the flesh is no longer translucent and separates easily with a fork.

For moist thick salmon, use gentler heat and a thermometer:

1. Salt the fillets briefly before cooking.
2. Bake, poach, steam, or sear then finish over lower heat.
3. Check temperature in the thickest part.
4. Let carryover finish the last bit of cooking instead of blasting the outside.

Quality cues are supporting evidence, not a replacement for safety: the fish should turn opaque, separate in large moist flakes, and feel just firm rather than chalky. For pregnant, older, immunocompromised, or otherwise higher-risk diners, stay conservative and use thermometer verification.
