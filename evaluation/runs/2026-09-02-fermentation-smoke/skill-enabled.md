## EVAL-022-low-salt-sauerkraut

Safety first: do not halve the required salt in a sauerkraut fermentation unless you have a tested reduced-salt recipe.

Current state: a preservation recipe is being changed for sodium. Target state: a lower-sodium cabbage product that is still safe. Mechanism: salt favors the desired fermentation organisms, inhibits others, draws cabbage juice into brine, and protects texture.

Lower-risk options:

1. Use a tested lower-sodium fermentation recipe from an authoritative preservation source.
2. Make a refrigerated quick pickle instead of a fermented sauerkraut.
3. Keep the tested fermentation recipe and use smaller portions, or rinse briefly at service if acceptable.

Do not treat salt as flavor only. For medical sodium limits, label the sodium uncertainty and avoid claiming the product fits a diet without the actual sodium target.

## EVAL-023-soft-fermented-pickles

Safety decision: discard them. Do not rinse, taste, or keep fermenting.

Observed state: soft, slippery cucumber pickles with an unpleasant smell. Target state: avoid eating a spoiled ferment. Mechanism: this can come from weak brine, high fermentation temperature, inadequate brine coverage, poor cucumber quality, or spoilage organisms. Rinsing removes surface residue but does not reverse microbial growth or texture breakdown inside the pickle.

Next batch controls:

1. Use a tested fermented-pickle recipe.
2. Keep cucumbers fully submerged under brine with a clean weight.
3. Use the specified salt and vinegar proportions.
4. Keep the ferment in the recommended temperature range.
5. Start with fresh cucumbers and remove blossom ends when the recipe calls for it.

Good signs are a clean sour smell, active bubbling early on, and firm texture. Slime, unpleasant odor, or soft slippery texture is a discard cue.

## EVAL-024-kombucha-bottle-pressure

Safety first: a bulging cap means pressure is already high. Do not continue warm room-temperature bottle fermentation.

Mechanism: sugar plus active microbes continue producing carbon dioxide in a sealed bottle. Warm storage and excess priming sugar or fruit accelerate pressure buildup.

Current batch:

1. If the bottles can be handled without obvious glass damage, chill them to slow fermentation.
2. Open only when cold, pointed away from people, over a sink or outside, and with protection from spraying liquid.
3. Discard any bottle that looks damaged, leaks, or seems unsafe to handle.

Next batch:

1. Confirm starter and process guidance from a reliable kombucha recipe.
2. Keep second fermentation shorter and use less sugar or fruit.
3. Use pressure-rated bottles, leave headspace, monitor carbonation, and refrigerate promptly.
4. Do not store finished kombucha in uncontrolled airtight containers.

Verification cue: no bulging caps, no damaged glass, controlled fizz after chilling, and prompt refrigeration after fermentation.

## EVAL-025-hot-sauce-fermentation-plan

Plan controls before flavor tweaks.

Safety gate: this is a home fermentation and storage question, so use a tested recipe and keep `food-safety` active. Active fermentation is not the same thing as shelf-stable bottling.

Set these controls:

1. Recipe: use tested salt, water, vinegar, and pepper proportions from an authoritative preservation source.
2. Vessel: use clean food-safe glass or food-grade plastic with enough headspace for gas.
3. Brine: keep peppers submerged with a clean weight.
4. Temperature and time: follow the recipe range and mark the start date.
5. Monitoring: watch brine coverage, bubbling, aroma, mold, slime, and pressure.
6. Storage: refrigerate the finished sauce or use only tested processing directions for shelf-stable storage.

Clean sour aroma and steady bubbling are expected. Fuzzy mold, rotten odor, slime, swollen sealed containers, or uncertain process history are stop-and-discard or source-check cues.
